# Introduction

## Problem Statement
Nowadays, many networks are subject to cyberattacks in which attackers gain access and interfere with the network maliciously. Network intrusion is a well-known cybersecurity threat, and with ongoing technological advances, many machine-learning-based models have been increasingly used and extensively trained to distinguish between normal and malicious traffic. However, the performance of such models depends heavily on the quality of the data they are trained on, making them vulnerable to data poisoning and cyberattacks. One such attack is label flipping, in which an attacker manipulates the training data by switching the labels of some samples, such as labeling attack traffic as benign or benign traffic as attack. This can cause the model to learn incorrect classifications and patterns, affecting its performance by reducing its ability to identify malicious traffic accurately.

## Motivation
These reduced detection capabilities can have serious consequences for individuals and organizations, as network intrusion attacks may go undetected. Through these label-flipping attacks, other cyberattacks can bypass the system, allowing for unauthorized access to the system, stealing sensitive information, disrupting services, and causing data breaches. Because of this, ML-based Network Intrusion Detectors need to be accurate and reliable so that organizations and individuals can use them efficiently without letting malicious intrusion attempts bypass detection. Therefore, analyzing the impact of label-flipping attacks on ML-based NIDS is crucial for understanding how vulnerable they are to these threats and how their performance and capabilities can be affected. This can further help us identify ways to make them more resistant to such attacks, improving robustness and reliability.

## Scope 
This study examines the effects of bidirectional label-flipping attacks on centralized ML-based Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS). The system will be trained using the publicly available UNSW-NB15 dataset for binary classification, where each network traffic sample is classified as either Attack (1) or Benign (0).  Random bidirectional label-flipping attacks will be simulated at different poisoning rates, including 10%, 20%, and 30% of the training set, to assess their impact on the effectiveness of the models in distinguishing between benign and malicious traffic. The study will focus on Random Forest and Decision Tree classifiers and will evaluate their performance on the clean dataset, poisoned dataset, and after applying the proposed defense mechanism. Additionally, metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, false-negative rate, and false-positive rate will be used for evaluation. The study, however, is limited to controlled experiments using the selected classifiers, specified attack settings, and the UNSW-NB15 dataset. It focuses only on centralized machine learning while excluding federated and distributed learning approaches from the project's scope.

## Relevance to Real-World Cybersecurity Practice
Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) are vital tools for identifying network attacks conveyed by malicious network traffic. ML-based NIDS can learn patterns from the previously labelled network traffic in the training dataset and use them to distinguish between benign and malicious traffic [1]. This indicates that training data plays a vital role and needs to be kept intact and safe for reliable intrusion detection. Attackers have gotten more advanced in this day and age, leveraging newly developed technologies like deep learning and machine learning to carry out malicious activities. Data poisoning attacks such as label-flipping could affect the system’s ability to detect future attacks, leading to undetected attacks or increased false alarms [1].
Studying the effects of label-flipping attacks can therefore help cybersecurity professionals better understand the system’s vulnerabilities. Similarly, evaluating the proposed defense mechanisms against these attacks may also help in developing more reliable NIDS that can maintain good performance and remain robust even against such attacks where part of the system is manipulated.
