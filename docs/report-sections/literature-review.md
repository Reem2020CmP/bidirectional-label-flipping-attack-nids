The most frequently used datasets in the reviewed papers were UNSW-NB15, CICIDS2017, CSE-CICIDS2018, and NSL-KDD [2]-[13], which were used to evaluate models in the NIDS domain. These studies will help investigate and analyze the effects of label-flipping attacks and the effectiveness of the proposed defense mechanisms.

## Attacks
Several papers investigate the effects of label-flipping attacks on machine learning models. This includes Dunn et al. [2], who tested different label-flipping poisoning ratios ranging from 0% to 30%. Their results showed that as the poisoning rates increased, model performance decreased across several classifiers, including Random Forest, Naïve Bayes, and others, with precision reaching as low as 47.1%.
Other papers, such as Paracha et al. [8], showed that the Random Forest model was more resilient, experiencing only an 8.5% decrease in accuracy, while Decision Tree had a 14.85% increase in false-positive rate at a 15% poisoning rate in UNSW-NB15. KNN also experienced a 15.48% decrease in accuracy at a 25% poisoning rate on BotDroid. These findings demonstrate that different models are affected differently by poisoned labels, showing their vulnerabilities. The decision tree was affected because corrupted labels influenced its splitting decisions, while KNN was affected by corrupted density classes. Mohammadian et al. [5] also showed that the accuracy of a Deep Neural Network (DNN) decreased from 97% to 29% at a 70% label-flipping poisoning rate, supporting the conclusion that label-flipping attacks can significantly degrade the performance of machine learning models.
Label-flipping attacks have also been investigated in federated learning settings, where models are trained across multiple clients and later aggregated together. Nowroozi et al. [7] demonstrated that label-flipping attacks caused a significant drop in accuracy, making the network server raise a critical error and easily detect it. Similarly, Lavaur et al. [10] showed that model accuracy could decrease to approximately 40-50%, demonstrating that federated learning systems can also experience critical performance degradation.

## Defenses
The study by Lai et al. [6] proposes a two-phase defense mechanism against label-flipping attacks in federated learning-based intrusion detection. The first phase identifies the poisoned client models by comparing their model weights. The second phase further identifies the poisoned models based on the performance of the aggregated global model. Results of this defense system show that the proposed defense improved performance by 3 % compared with other existing defense mechanisms.
Other studies, such as Sameera et al. [12], proposed a defense called WeiDetect, which detects label-flipping attacks by evaluating local models using validation sets, then putting the results into a probability for analyzing outliers, making it exclude the low-performance models that were attacked. Their results showed improvements of up to 70% in recall and between 1% and 14% in F1-score by 1- 14% compared with existing defenses, showing its efficiency.
Another paper [3] proposes SecFedNIDS, which detects poisoned models based on model parameters and uses unsupervised poisoned model detection to reject malicious models. It also identifies poisoned data through class-path similarity, improving accuracy by 13%. Luong et al. [14] proposed FedLSAE, which uses an autoencoder that compresses the features of the training data and checks for similarities to group them. The method was tested on two IoT cyberattack datasets using several ML models. Other studies, such as Rezai et al. [9], proposed GRAF-IDS, which uses an enhanced version of the FedAVG method, making aggregation through graph-based clustering, resulting in lower false positive rates.
For centralized learning, Jang et al. [13] use KNN sanitization together with a self-supervised learning model, BYOL, as a defense against label-flipping attacks. The BYOL model identifies the features and learns their patterns; then this labeled representation is analyzed using KNN, which uses clustering to detect outliers in class behavior and determine whether a sample’s label disagrees with the labels of its nearest neighbors. Suspicious samples are then removed before model training. Their results showed that there was an improvement with a 0.779 F1-score in random label flipping attacks and 0.701 accuracy in decision boundary label flipping attacks.
Of particular relevance, He et al. [11] proposed TE-PADN, which uses temporal margin sample generation with an attention mechanism that generates these samples to fix poisoned models. They start by collecting the infected samples, then putting them into a model that has an attention mechanism which generates synthetic samples. These samples represent data points that are close to the true decision boundary. Then they fix the decision boundary that had shifted during the label-flipping attack by introducing these synthetic samples into the training dataset, which pulls the decision boundary back to its correct position. Their results showed an increase in accuracy by 6.5%, proving their defense mechanism effective. 
 
## Research Gaps
Several of the reviewed studies mainly investigate the impact of label-flipping attacks on ML-based models without implementing defense mechanisms. These studies demonstrate how poisoning attacks affect different classifiers but do not focus on methods for identifying or correcting poisoned labels. Therefore, investigating a defense mechanism is important to mitigate the problem with the help of the defense papers used [2], [5], [7], [8], [10]. 
Other reviewed studies proposed defense mechanisms, specifically in federated learning settings, using techniques such as model aggregation, clustering, validation-based detection, and poisoned client rejection. However, these techniques are mainly designed for federated environments and might not directly address poisoned training samples in a centralized setting [3], [4], [6], [9], [12], [14].
In centralized learning, KNN was used as a technique to identify suspicious samples based on neighborhood label disagreement. However, relying on this approach alone might not provide sufficient evidence that a label was poisoned. Additionally, some reviewed approaches remove suspicious samples rather than correcting them. Removing a large number of samples, specifically under higher poisoning rates, will reduce the amount of training data available for the model, possibly affecting its performance [13]. Therefore, this introduces an opportunity to study a hybrid defense mechanism that combines neighborhood-based and model-based findings that might provide us with stronger evidence for identifying suspicious labels and analyzing their impact on classification performance.
